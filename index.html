Strategic career advisory for experienced professionals.

FAQ

Common questions about career strategy.

Straight answers to what experienced professionals usually ask before their first session.

What is executive career advisory?

Executive career advisory is strategic work focused on deciding what your next career move should be — and how to position and execute it — rather than generic interview or resume coaching. It starts from your existing professional capital and maps the realistic paths it can open.

How is career advisory different from career coaching?

Career coaching typically helps with skills like interviewing, networking, or resume writing. Career strategy addresses an earlier question: which direction is worth pursuing at all. For experienced professionals, the harder problem is usually deciding, not executing.

Is it too late to change careers in your 40s or 50s?

Rarely, from a capability standpoint. What changes with experience is the type of transition that makes sense — a senior professional's accumulated expertise, network, results, and reputation usually transfers better through repositioning than through starting over.

How can an executive transition into independent consulting?

The transition works best when it starts from a clear view of which parts of your operating experience are in demand as advisory value, not from repackaging a job title. That typically means defining a narrow expertise area, building a positioning narrative, and using an existing network before any market outreach.

How do executives find board or advisory positions?

Board and advisory roles are rarely won through open applications — they're built through visible expertise, existing relationships, and a clear point of view in a specific domain. Combining an operating role with board or advisory work is often the realistic first step, rather than a full-time board career.

What should I do if my career has plateaued?

A plateau is often a career inflection point rather than a dead end: a moment where continuing on inertia has a real opportunity cost. The useful first step is mapping your accumulated professional capital and the options it currently supports, before deciding on a direction.

How do I identify which parts of my experience are transferable to a new industry?

Focus on what generalizes: leadership scope, the type of problems you've solved, and measurable results — rather than industry-specific terminology. Industry knowledge is real value, but it's rarely the only value a senior professional carries between sectors.
